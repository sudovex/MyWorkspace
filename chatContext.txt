## The core mental model

This is not one app. It's **two runtimes fused at a single seam**:

1. A **WebGL runtime** (the room) — GPU-bound, imperative, driven by a render loop.
2. A **DOM runtime** (ShubhamOS) — a windowing environment that needs real DOM for dragging, text input, forms, and accessibility.

Almost every hard decision flows from managing that boundary. The "magical" monitor transition is the seam where control hands off from one runtime to the other, and the whole thing is governed by a single experience-level **finite state machine**.

## Experience state machine

Everything the visitor does is a transition in one FSM. This is the backbone — build it first.

```
BOOT ─▶ APPROACHING ─▶ THROUGH_GLASS ─▶ ROOM_IDLE
                                            │  (mouse moves)
                                            ▼
                                       MONITOR_WAKING ─▶ MONITOR_READY
                                            │  (click monitor)
                                            ▼
                                        ZOOMING ─────▶ OS_ACTIVE
                                            ▲               │ (exit / "leave desk")
                                            └───────────────┘
```

`OS_ACTIVE` is the critical performance state: once we're in the OS, the WebGL canvas freezes (or unmounts) so the browser spends zero GPU on the room while someone reads your projects.

## The transition (the part everyone gets wrong)

The wrong approach — rendering the OS to a texture inside the 3D monitor via CSS3D/html-to-canvas — kills interactivity and performance. Don't.

The right approach: the OS is a **real DOM layer** sitting above the canvas. During `ZOOMING`, GSAP flies the camera to frame the monitor, and at the climax a DOM container — CSS-positioned and scaled to exactly overlay the monitor's screen rect — cross-fades and scales up to fill the viewport. As it lands, we flip to `OS_ACTIVE` and freeze the canvas. Reverse the timeline to go back. Feels seamless, stays fully interactive and accessible, and lets the GPU rest.

## Performance strategy (and an honest caveat)

Concrete levers: `frameloop="demand"` in the room (only render on interaction/animation) and hard-freeze in OS mode; Draco/meshopt-compressed GLTF; KTX2/Basis compressed textures; `AdaptiveDpr` + `PerformanceMonitor` to drop resolution under load; route-level code splitting so the 3D libs never block the OS and vice versa; lazy-loaded apps and sandbox demos.

The caveat you should hear from an architect and not discover at launch: **a rain-and-PBR cinematic 3D scene will not hit Lighthouse Performance > 90 on throttled mobile.** That target and a heavy WebGL landing are in genuine tension. The realistic plan is a **device-tiered experience**: detect capability up front, serve the full scene to capable devices, and give low-power/mobile/reduced-motion visitors a lightweight "poster" entry (static hero image → straight into the OS). That protects both the numbers and the visitor.

## SEO and accessibility (designed in, not bolted on)

Crawlers and screen readers see nothing inside a WebGL canvas, so SEO > 95 and Accessibility > 95 are impossible without a plan. Solution: **server-render a real, semantic HTML version of your content** (projects, experience, contact) that's visually hidden but present in the DOM and metadata. It satisfies crawlers, gives assistive tech a genuine path, and doubles as the reduced-motion fallback. `prefers-reduced-motion` skips the cinematic intro entirely. This is why local typed content matters — you can't SSR content that lives in a client-side DB call.

## Architectural decisions

**Camera poses are data, not baked into the rig** (`poses.ts`). The pullback and the OS-entry zoom are literally the same two anchors run in opposite directions, so M2's boot choreography and M4's zoom become sequencing problems over shared data rather than bespoke animation code.

**The `AssetSlot` firewall.** Every object renders placeholder geometry inside an `<AssetSlot id=...>`. Swapping to a production model is a *data-only* edit — set `gltfPath` in the manifest and the slot renders the GLTF at the identical transform. Object components never learn whether they're a box or a real model, which is what keeps M3's interaction logic stable across the swap.

**Scene is dynamically imported with `ssr: false`.** WebGL never touches the server, and — visible in the build output — the entire Three.js bundle splits into a lazy chunk, keeping the initial route at ~114 kB. This is the scene/OS code-split from the M0 plan, now real.

**Device tier gates *whether* WebGL mounts at all**, not just quality. Low-tier devices get the DOM `StaticFallback` and never pay for a canvas — the honest answer to the Lighthouse-vs-cinematic tension I flagged in the architecture phase.

## Performance optimizations

`frameloop="demand"` is the big one: the `CameraRig` calls `invalidate()` only while the camera is still travelling and goes silent once settled, so a still room renders **zero frames**. `PerformanceMonitor` ratchets DPR down under GPU stress. DPR ceiling, shadow-map size, and antialias are all tier-scaled. Damping is framerate-independent (built on `THREE.MathUtils.damp`, no `maath` dependency added).

The placeholder room is deliberately *plain* — it exists to prove lighting, camera, and the slot system, not to look finished; it will look markedly better once real GLTF/HDRI assets land (all catalogued in `TODO_ASSETS.md`). Lighting is fully analytic right now (no HDRI environment map) specifically to avoid a network asset dependency during development — reflections get richer in M2. ## Architecture

**Golden rule:** `features/scene/` and `features/os/` never import each other. They talk only through `stores/experienceStore.ts`.

```
app/       routes, layout, providers, globals.css (design tokens)
config/    siteConfig — single source for SEO/OS chrome
content/   typed portfolio content (TS + MDX) — never a database
features/  scene/ (WebGL) · os/ (DOM) · easter-eggs/
hooks/     useDeviceTier, usePrefersReducedMotion
lib/       utils, env (Zod), supabase client/server factories
stores/    experience (FSM) · window · os · terminal
types/     framework-free domain models
```

**The FSM** is the spine. `black → post → booting → monitor_on → reveal → room_idle → zooming → os_active`, with a guarded transition table (`PHASE_TRANSITIONS`) and a pure, separately-tested `canTransition`. ## The critique

**Camera.** Exponential damping starts at maximum velocity and decelerates. That's a UI transition curve, not a dolly curve — physical cameras have mass and ease *in* as well as out. Position and look-at share one lambda and arrive simultaneously, which is the classic robotic-CG tell; real operators lead with one and lag the other. And it dead-stops. No residual float, no breathing.

**Lighting.** This is the fatal one. Every light in the room is at full intensity on frame zero. There is not a single lighting *event* in the entire sequence. The room is a lit diorama and the camera is a tour guide walking you around it. Your instinct is exactly right: light should reveal the world, and right now light does nothing at all.

**Motion quality.** One curve, applied uniformly, with no secondary motion, no overshoot, no settle. Everything moves in perfect lockstep, which nothing in the physical world ever does.

**Storytelling.** Zero. The FSM *contains* a narrative — black, POST, booting — and none of it is on screen. Nothing is asserted about who this is, why it's night, or why the machine was off.

**Overlap is the whole game.** The failure mode of the sequence as you sketched it is that it's a *list* — beats in a list happen one after another; beats in a film bleed into each other.
